Hashline: cryptographic verification of AI model runs, without disclosing the data

Victor Rielly · victor23@pdx.edu · Portland, Oregon, USA · linkedin.com/in/victor-rielly-50508b3a

1. The work, and the problem it addresses

Agreements and audits about advanced AI depend on claims that outsiders cannot check:

  • which model was evaluated, and what it scored;
  • whether the model deployed is the one that was audited;
  • what a safety filter decided.

Today a checker must trust the operator, see the private data (user content, test sets, prompts), or rely on secure hardware, which moves the trust to a chip vendor and its supply chain. None of these scales to verification between parties that do not trust each other, such as labs, auditors, insurers or governments.

Hashline is a zero-knowledge prover for transformer inference.

  • What a proof shows: a stated model, run on a hidden input, produced a stated result: a next token, a classifier's verdict, or an evaluation score.
  • Who can check it: anyone.
  • What stays hidden: the input, the output and every intermediate activation. The verifier learns only the facts the owner chooses to publish.
  • What it rests on: a standard hash function. There is no trusted setup and no secure hardware, and soundness is counted at about 97 bits per run.

What works today [measured, single runs unless noted]:

  • Trained Llama-3-8B, private next token: proved in 192 s on a laptop CPU, verified in 43 s. 65 of 65 tampered proofs were rejected.
  • A real 3B content-safety classifier (Mistral's Shieldstral): private proofs of its verdicts on a post, all 26 layers, anchored on a public testnet.
  • A new GPU backend: one 8B-sized layer over 128 hidden tokens proves in 0.36 s on one Apple GPU, down from 12 s on a laptop CPU. That is about 11 proved tokens per second for a full 32-layer model (computed from the per-layer time). The target is 50.
  • Privacy: two privacy statements accepted after independent internal reviews. There has been no external audit yet.

The 12-month plan:

  1. NVIDIA GPUs: port the GPU prover to NVIDIA (RTX 5090, then H100), with proofs byte-identical to the CPU reference. Target: 50 proved tokens per second for an 8B model on one card. Throughput is measured and published either way.
  2. Open release: release the whole stack under a permissive licence: prover, verifier, a specification of the proof system and its privacy statement, and reproducible benchmarks.
  3. External review: commission an external cryptographic review of soundness and zero-knowledge, and publish it with our fixes.
  4. Verification uses:
    • proofs of evaluation scores on hidden test sets, so an evaluator can publish a checkable score while keeping its test items secret;
    • proofs of which model produced an output.
  5. Hidden weights (version 2): design it, so a lab can prove facts about a confidential model. That is the step that verification of frontier systems needs.

2. Background, what I have built, and why AI safety matters to me

  • Education: PhD in Applied Mathematics (Portland State University, 2025, machine-learning emphasis); B.S. in Mathematics and Physics (Pacific University, salutatorian).
  • Research: papers in the Journal of Computational Physics and TMLR, a third accepted in a SIAM journal and two under review. They are on kernel methods that learn differential equations from data. I am a postdoctoral researcher at the Translational Genomics Research Institute.
  • Earlier work: R&D at Intel and NASA, and three years as lead engineer of a networked IoT device.
  • Hashline: since December 2025 I have led its design, made every security and privacy ruling, and directed a team of AI coding agents under strict rules: byte-identical outputs, independent soundness and privacy reviews of every release, and a recorded measurement behind every number. That is about 1,640 commits.

Why AI safety: as AI systems take on decisions that matter, the ability to check what they did, without surveillance and without blind trust, becomes basic infrastructure. In 2023 I wrote a research proposal on provably safe language models. Hashline is that interest made concrete. I would rather the verification layer of AI governance be open and reviewable than owned by one company. The only fast competitor I know of is closed source.
